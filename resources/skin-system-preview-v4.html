Which is the largest organ of the human body?

Your move. Lock in an answer to open the evidence file.

Evidence file opened

The answer is skin—but the system is larger.

Skin is the body’s largest organ by surface area and weight. Together with hair, nails, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands, it forms the integumentary system.

Integumentary system

Integument means a covering. This organ system forms the living boundary between the body and the outside world.

Epidermis

the outer barrier

Essential clue

The epidermis has no blood vessels. Most of its cells are keratinocytes, which make the tough protein keratin and continually renew the barrier.

The hypodermis lies beneath the skin. Its fat and connective tissue cushion, insulate, and anchor the skin to deeper structures.

Supporting structures

The parts people notice—and their scientific names

Many familiar skin features are appendages: structures formed from epidermal tissue but extending into the dermis.

Sweat gland

Sudoriferous gland

An exocrine gland that releases sweat through a duct. Eccrine glands open onto the skin and cool the body. Apocrine glands empty into hair follicles in selected areas and become active at puberty.

Exocrine, not endocrine: exocrine glands use ducts; endocrine glands release hormones into the bloodstream.
Oil gland

Sebaceous gland

Usually opens into a hair follicle and releases sebum, an oily mixture that lubricates hair and skin and contributes to the surface barrier.

Hair root

Hair follicle

A tube-like pocket extending into the dermis. The hair bulb contains actively dividing matrix cells, while the tiny arrector pili muscle produces goosebumps.

Touch and pain sensors

Cutaneous receptors

Free nerve endings and specialized receptors detect pain, temperature, pressure, vibration, and light touch.

Fingernail or toenail

Nail plate

A hard sheet of keratin produced by the nail matrix. It protects the fingertip and provides counterpressure for fine touch and grasping.

Defence cells

Immune cells

Epidermal dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, and other immune cells detect damage, signal danger, and help coordinate inflammation and repair.

Adipose tissue

Connective tissue made mainly of fat cells; stores energy, insulates, and cushions.

Apocrine gland

A sweat gland that empties into a hair follicle in areas such as the armpit and becomes active at puberty.

Capillary

The smallest type of blood vessel; exchanges oxygen, nutrients, heat, and wastes with tissue.

Collagen

A structural protein that gives connective tissue tensile strength and forms much of a scar.

Dermis

The vascular connective-tissue layer supporting the epidermis and containing nerves, follicles, and glands.

Eccrine gland

A widespread sweat gland that opens at the skin surface and primarily supports cooling.

Epidermis

The avascular, keratinized outer layer of skin.

Exocrine gland

A gland that sends its product through a duct to a surface or cavity.

Fibroblast

A connective-tissue cell that produces collagen and other extracellular-matrix components.

Hypodermis

The tissue below the dermis that anchors and cushions skin; it is not technically part of the skin.

Keratin

A tough structural protein abundant in the epidermis, hair, and nails.

Keratinocyte

The main epidermal cell; produces keratin and helps build the barrier.

Melanin

A pigment transferred to keratinocytes that contributes to colour and reduces UV-related DNA damage.

Sebaceous gland

An exocrine gland that releases oily sebum, usually into a hair follicle.

Sebum

The oily product of sebaceous glands that lubricates skin and hair.

Stratum corneum

The outermost epidermal layer, made of flattened, keratin-rich cells within a lipid barrier.
