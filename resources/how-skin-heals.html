How does skin heal?

Follow the body from broken barrier to strengthened scar—and discover the cells coordinating the repair.

Suggested levelGrades 7–9
Core learning time45–60 minutes
Subject connectionsBiology · Health
Prior knowledgeCells and body systems
Essential question

How does the body restore a damaged protective barrier, and why do some wounds heal better than others?

Learning goals

By the end

  • Explain how the epidermis, dermis and subcutaneous tissue contribute to protection and repair.
  • Connect specialized cells to their roles in clotting, defence, rebuilding and strengthening.
  • Describe the four overlapping phases of wound healing.
  • Use evidence from a wound scenario to predict what the body is doing next.

Pathway 1

Explore the skin

Core lesson

Skin is an organ, not wrapping paper. Its layers work together to control water loss, sense the environment, regulate temperature and block many physical, chemical and biological threats.

Epidermis

Most epidermal cells are keratinocytes. New keratinocytes form near the base, mature as they move upward and help create a water-resistant outer barrier. The epidermis has no blood vessels, so a very shallow scrape may ooze tissue fluid without much bleeding.

Partial-thickness injury

Some skin structures remain. Cells can migrate from wound edges and surviving structures to help cover the surface.

Full-thickness injury

All skin layers are disrupted. Repair must fill from deeper tissue and move inward from the edges, increasing healing time and scarring risk.

Homeostasis

Closing the wound helps restore stable temperature, fluid balance and protection from microorganisms.

Vocabulary: barrier, keratinocyte, collagen, extracellular matrix, partial thickness, full thickness, homeostasis.
Check your reasoning: Why does a paper cut bleed when the epidermis has no blood vessels?
A paper cut usually reaches through the epidermis into the vascular dermis. Blood comes from damaged dermal capillaries, not from the epidermis itself.

Pathway 2

Meet the repair team

Deep dive

No single cell “heals” a wound. Different cells arrive, communicate and change the environment so the next part of repair can occur.

Immediate response

Platelets

Stick to damaged vessels, help build a clot and release chemical signals called growth factors. The clot limits blood loss and forms a temporary scaffold.

Early defence

Neutrophils

Move rapidly into injured tissue. They attack microorganisms and help clear damaged material, but excessive or prolonged activity can also injure healthy tissue.

Cleanup and coordination

Macrophages

Engulf debris and microorganisms, then release signals that help shift the wound from inflammation toward rebuilding.

Rebuilding

Fibroblasts

Produce collagen and other extracellular-matrix materials that support new tissue. Some help contract the wound edges.

Surface closure

Keratinocytes

Divide and migrate across the wound surface in a process called re-epithelialization, restoring the protective barrier.

New circulation

Endothelial cells

Line new capillaries as they grow into the repair tissue. This angiogenesis supplies oxygen and nutrients to metabolically active cells.

Vocabulary: growth factor, phagocytosis, fibroblast, extracellular matrix, re-epithelialization, angiogenesis.
Check your reasoning: Why could poor circulation slow several repair jobs at once?
Repairing tissue needs oxygen and nutrients. Reduced circulation can limit immune defence, collagen production, cell division and new-tissue growth rather than affecting only one phase.

Pathway 3

Follow the healing process

Core + clinical connection

The phases are named separately so we can study them, but biology does not wait for one phase to finish before beginning another.

1. Hemostasis

Begins immediately. Vessels constrict, platelets gather and clotting proteins stabilize a fibrin mesh.

2. Inflammation

Usually dominates early days. Neutrophils and macrophages defend and clear the wound while chemical signals coordinate repair.

3. Proliferation

Builds new tissue. Granulation tissue forms, capillaries grow, collagen is deposited and keratinocytes cover the surface.

4. Remodelling

May continue for months or longer. Collagen is reorganized and the scar becomes stronger, although it does not fully match uninjured skin.

Hemostasis
Inflammation
Proliferation
Remodelling

Conceptual timeline only. Wound depth, location, infection, circulation and health can change the pace.

Vocabulary: hemostasis, fibrin, inflammation, granulation tissue, proliferation, remodelling, tensile strength.
Apply it: A healing scrape is pink and slightly raised, with fragile new surface tissue. Which processes are likely active?
Proliferation is prominent: keratinocytes are closing the surface, fibroblasts are depositing matrix and new capillaries can make granulation tissue appear pink. Inflammation may still overlap, and early remodelling may be beginning.

From biology to care

Protect the repair zone

🚿

Rinse

Wash your hands first. For a minor cut or scrape, control bleeding with gentle pressure, then hold the wound under cool, clean running water to remove dirt and debris.

🩹

Cover

A clean dressing protects against rubbing and contamination while new surface cells restore the barrier. Replace it when wet or soiled.

👀

Observe

Normal inflammation should trend toward improvement. Spreading redness, worsening pain, pus, fever or red streaks can suggest infection.

Seek medical assessment for bleeding that will not stop, a deep or gaping wound, embedded material, an animal or human bite, loss of sensation or movement, or possible infection. Dirty or deep wounds may also require a tetanus assessment.
